# Research & Insight Foundation

**State:** Meghalaya, India
**Niche:** The living root bridges of the Khasi and Jaintia hills — architecture that is grown rather than constructed.
**Deliverable:** D02 · Satisfies R04, R06, R18, R19, R03

---

## 1. Why this state, and why this niche

Meghalaya is normally sold on waterfalls, caves and viewpoints. That is sightseeing, and the brief rules it out. Underneath the postcard version sits something with no equivalent anywhere in the world.

In the Khasi and Jaintia hills, villagers train the aerial roots of the rubber fig tree across rivers until the roots thicken, knit and become a footbridge. The Khasi term is *jingkieng jri*. The practice is living structural engineering carried out with no drawings, no contractors and no completion date.

Four properties make this a genuine niche rather than a scenic attraction:

1. **It is alive.** The structure is a growing organism, not a built object.
2. **It is never finished.** A crossing becomes usable after roughly a decade and a half to three decades, and it keeps thickening for a century or more afterwards.
3. **It gets stronger with age.** Steel rusts and timber rots in this climate. These crossings do the opposite — they gain load capacity as they mature.
4. **Nobody who starts one uses it.** The person who first trains the roots is generally not the person who walks across. Each crossing is begun for a stranger who does not exist yet.

The region sits among the wettest inhabited places on earth. That context matters: this is not craft for its own sake. Conventional bridges fail there. The living solution was adopted because it is the one that survives.

> **Verification note.** Ages, spans and counts vary by source and by structure. Specific figures used in any public-facing execution must be confirmed with the Meghalaya Tourism Department and with village councils before release. Nothing in this campaign depends on a contested number.

## 2. Target group

**Primary: urban Indian knowledge workers, 28–42, in the major metros.**

Not chosen by income bracket, which tells us nothing useful. Chosen by a shared working condition.

These people work in software, marketing, media, content, product and finance. They are competent and well paid. And almost nothing they make outlasts two years. Code is refactored. Campaigns run and are archived. Decks are presented once. Their professional output is designed to be replaced, and they know it.

**The insight:**

> They spend their working lives making things that are obsolete before they are finished, and they have never once made something that will outlive them.

This is the gap ordinary tourism advertising cannot reach. It does not offer rest, escape, luxury or adventure — all of which this audience has already bought and found temporary. It offers the one thing their working life structurally denies them: **participation in something slower than their own career.**

**Secondary: international slow-travel and craft-heritage travellers**, principally from the UK, Germany, the Netherlands and Japan. They already seek out living traditions and intergenerational craft. They are a natural fit but they are not the group the campaign is built for, because they are already persuaded.

**Who this campaign is deliberately not for:** the itinerary tourist collecting locations. The proposition actively repels them, which is correct — the villages involved cannot absorb volume, and the campaign should not create demand the place cannot carry.

---

## 3. The USP

> **Meghalaya is the only place on earth where the landmark you came to see is not finished, and where you can add to it.**

Every competing destination sells a finished object: a monument, a beach, a skyline. Those can only be looked at. A living crossing is mid-construction, permanently, and the construction method is something an ordinary visitor can genuinely contribute a few minutes of work to under guidance.

This converts the visitor from audience into participant. That is the strategic hinge of the entire campaign.

## 4. Competitive context

| Destination | What it sells | Its ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Kerala | Backwaters, wellness, cuisine | Restorative but passive; the visitor consumes |
| Rajasthan | Palaces, heritage, spectacle | Finished objects, viewed from outside |
| Northeast circuit generally | Unspoilt scenery, tribal culture | Sold as remoteness; visitor is an observer |
| Bali, Bhutan | Spiritual reset, slow living | Rest without contribution; you leave nothing |
| **Meghalaya, this campaign** | **An unfinished structure you help continue** | **The visitor leaves something behind that grows** |

Nobody else can make this claim, because nobody else has architecture that is still being grown.
